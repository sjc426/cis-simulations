LEGAL FRAMEWORK — WHAT YOU CAN AND CANNOT DO

"The Golden Rule of Network Access: You may only access systems you own, or systems for which you have explicit written authorization. Everything else is a federal crime."

— CIS 256 Course Policy · Community College of Philadelphia
// federal_laws
FEDERAL LAWS YOU MUST KNOW
Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) — 18 U.S.C. § 1030

Criminalizes unauthorized access to computers and networks. Penalties range from 1 to 20 years depending on the offense. "I was just curious" is not a defense. "I didn't know it was unauthorized" is not a defense.

Wiretap Act — 18 U.S.C. § 2511

Prohibits intercepting electronic communications without authorization. Running Wireshark on a network you do not own or control — even passively — is a federal violation.

Electronic Communications Privacy Act (ECPA)

Extends wiretap protections to stored electronic communications and data. Accessing someone's stored emails, files, or network logs without authorization violates federal law.

Pennsylvania Computer Hacking Laws — 18 Pa. C.S. § 7611–7615

State-level offenses that mirror federal law. Pennsylvania prosecutes independently of federal charges — you can face both simultaneously.

// real_world_cases
REAL CASES — REAL CONSEQUENCES
Case 1: The Curious Student

A student ran nmap against their college's network "just to see what was open." IT flagged it within minutes. The student faced suspension and a referral to campus security — no criminal charges only because the college chose not to press them.

Case 2: The Wi-Fi Sniffer

A student ran Wireshark at a coffee shop capturing packets. Even without reading them, passive capture on a network you don't own violates the Wiretap Act. Federal charges were filed.

Case 3: The "Researcher"

A student scanned a company network to "report vulnerabilities." Without written authorization, this is unauthorized access under the CFAA regardless of intent. The student was prosecuted.
